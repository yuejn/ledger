3:T717,<p>Sparsely attended which was ironic (or... you know) given the main thesis of the night was that there aren&#39;t enough people in the west who are knowledgeable on the current nuclear situation. </p>
<p>The uncomfortable precise: the Cold War architecture that kept nuclear weapons in check (treaties, inspections) have expired. Now we are flying without any instruments. America&#39;s protective umbrella is rapidly decaying under Trump 2,0; and middle powers like the Netherlands which spent decades pushing for disarmament are edging back towards &quot;actually, maybe we need nuclear protection&quot;. It was a noted, significant shift. </p>
<p>The AI topic was where I started taking notes a bit faster. I was horrified at the bombing of an Iranian school -- and this was referenced in Shane Harris&#39; talk earlier on AI in war. The argument wasn&#39;t that algorithms will <em>launch</em> missiles -- that decision will (should) always remain with humans. What will happen (or is happening) is much more subtler: all the steps <em>leading up</em> to that moment are increasingly handled by machines and algorithms.</p>
<p>If I&#39;ve been worried about AI&#39;s impact on critical thinking and intellectual laziness, it&#39;s probably more apparent here.  What information exactly is the human with the big red button deciding with? You cannot, I reckon, outsource your thinking and then switch it back on for the HARDEST call imaginable. (NUC. LEAR. WEAP. ONS.)</p>
<p>The talk kept going back to &#39;ze Russians&#39;, as it were, and knowledge. Russia has a thriving community of nuclear strategists; the West has very, very, very few left. Someone said: go talk to the last generation who lived through deterrence as practitioners -- before they die. There was something so insane to hear that. </p>
