3:T961,<p>I loved Zabuzhko&#39;s energy and her give-absolutely-zero-fucks aura. She talked about how Ukrainians learned, over the generations, to make themselves invisible -- not to speak, not to show what you have, to survive by disappearing. Then she said, &quot;Hey, the brutal irony is that when you go quiet, someone else fills the silence for you&quot;, and -- as we know -- Russia did exactly that, writing in Ukraine&#39;s story as a failed state for decades. Ukrainians only started pushing back in 2014 which, historically speaking is &quot;five minutes ago&quot;.</p>
<p>There was also a Dutch-Ukrainian writer, Lisa Weeda, who spoke. Her angle was much more abrasive. She wasn&#39;t talking about Ukraine so much as talking about the West (or like, the Dutch, or &quot;us&quot; which was interesting because I am... not Dutch). Her thesis: the West has been handing Russia the microphone for so long that countries like Ukraine, Georgia, Moldova barely get a word in. Russia sees itself as an island with no outside word -- and yet we, the West, centre it in every conversation about the region. I think her speech was impassioned but a bit clumsy, but I liked her metaphor: the West has willingly been blindsided and other countries have lost oxygen. </p>
<p>What was a decent thread throughout all the speeches and discussion was the idea that erasure starts with language. We redraw the Iron Curtain every time we lazily use &quot;Eastern Europe&quot;.</p>
<p>One of the audience members asked: &quot;How can you talk to Dutch people about Ukraine against the immense amount of Russian propaganda?&quot; Zabuzhko said: forget out-arguing Russian propaganda -- you cannot outspend it. Go personal: a concert, a story, a book. She cited Notre Dame as an example -- it burns and the whole world mourns because it lives in people&#39;s personal heritage. The Sofia Cathedral in Kyiv is older and just as extraordinary but it doesn&#39;t live yet in the overall public consciousness. There is work to be done. </p>
<p>There were some hopeful notes to this session. Dutch universities are now teaching Ukrainian, Slavic studies are widening beyond their Russia-fixation, and there are organisations embedding Ukrainian culture into Dutch life. </p>
<p>European memory is slowly being redrawn towards community and self-determination; it is something being conscious of, of paying attention to.</p>
