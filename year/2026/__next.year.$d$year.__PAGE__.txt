4:Ta16,Simon, a pirate, gets elected as the Doge over the aristocracy's objections. However, he had a secret affair that produced a baby who disappeared ... and also that lady's dad is really upset with Simon. Lots of grudges, death, the potential for incest -- sorry, but this is an opera and you have to be prepared for all manner of things. However, the bulk of this opera is set twenty years later -- when he's trying to maintain cohesion and rule of law and he discovers his daughter who is, woe!, in love with one of his political rivals. 

A grand show -- Italian palazzo grandeur, crown moulding (I'm sorry but this has to be stated because it's so noticeable when there isn't ANY kind of detail in decor), textured beautiful walls -- a step up from some other designs I've seen. The visuals delivered throughout the night: windows that passed the day into the night; smoke and blinding lights (OK this I can give up); quick snazzy and smooth set changes that kept up the momentum. I'm a sucker for big choruses and this production delivered. 

Singing was really impressive across the board. Amelia's voice was piercing (damn girl). Fiesco's bass was moving. Adorno was a strong solo and harmonised really nicely with Amelia -- indicating that finally, they are Meant To Be, I guess. Paolo's villainy was a bit lol to me, mostly because the orchestra kept overshadowing him.

The hectic council chamber scene with the "kidnapper" getting cursed was a real highlight. I was in the nosebleeds so I lost detail in the facial expressions but the fear in the crowd madness vibe came through pretty loud and clear. 

Story-wise, I enjoyed and appreciated the forgiveness-across-generations arc. And the final scene of Simon dying on the same slab from the opening scene landed for me really well. Ik ben een easy audience. 

Less fun was the constant hand-wringing over Maria & Amelia's purity, raised in act one and again in act two at which point I was just like, "ugh guys" -- just made me think of how protecting a woman's chastity has always been a mechanism of controlling us.

The ending ran a liiiiiittle too long, but it was also really warm in the theatre and I was sitting right in between two people who kept coughing (an observation, I know no one WANTS to cough in the theatre); and Paolo just vanishes off to the scaffold with zero ceremony. I think the final scenes were staged wonderfully, with the city all dressed in black -- mourners, little angels of death calling Simon, the gravity of his fate. 

Minor gripes aside, I would recommend it. The uncomfortable precise: the Cold War architecture that kept nuclear weapons in check (treaties, inspections) have expired. Now we are flying without any instruments. The AI topic was where I started taking notes a bit faster. I was horrified at the bombing of an Iranian school -- and this was referenced in Shane Harris' talk earlier on AI in war. The argument wasn't that algorithms will _launch_ missiles -- that decision will (should) always remain with humans. What will happen (or is happening) is much more subtler: all the steps _leading up_ to that moment are increasingly handled by machines and algorithms.

If I've been worried about AI's impact on critical thinking and intellectual laziness, it's probably more apparent here.  What information exactly is the human with the big red button deciding with? You cannot, I reckon, outsource your thinking and then switch it back on for the HARDEST call imaginable. (NUC. LEAR. WEAP. ONS.)

The talk kept going back to 'ze Russians', as it were, and knowledge. Russia has a thriving community of nuclear strategists; the West has very, very, very few left. Someone said: go talk to the last generation who lived through deterrence as practitioners -- before they die. There was something so insane to hear that.

There was also a Dutch-Ukrainian writer, Lisa Weeda, who spoke. Her angle was much more abrasive. Her thesis: the West has been handing Russia the microphone for so long that countries like Ukraine, Georgia, Moldova barely get a word in. Russia sees itself as an island with no outside word -- and yet we, the West, centre it in every conversation about the region. I think her speech was impassioned but a bit clumsy, but I liked her metaphor: the West has willingly been blindsided and other countries have lost oxygen. 

What was a decent thread throughout all the speeches and discussion was the idea that erasure starts with language. We redraw the Iron Curtain every time we lazily use "Eastern Europe".

One of the audience members asked: "How can you talk to Dutch people about Ukraine against the immense amount of Russian propaganda?" Zabuzhko said: forget out-arguing Russian propaganda -- you cannot outspend it. Go personal: a concert, a story, a book. She cited Notre Dame as an example -- it burns and the whole world mourns because it lives in people's personal heritage. The Sofia Cathedral in Kyiv is older and just as extraordinary but it doesn't live yet in the overall public consciousness. There is work to be done. 

There were some hopeful notes to this session. Dutch universities are now teaching Ukrainian, Slavic studies are widening beyond their Russia-fixation, and there are organisations embedding Ukrainian culture into Dutch life.
